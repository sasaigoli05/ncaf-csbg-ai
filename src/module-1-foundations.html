Module 1 · Foundations of Artificial Intelligence

It predicts. It does not know.

Before you can use an AI tool well, you need an accurate picture of what it is doing when it answers you. That picture explains everything else — why it is fast, why it is useful, and why it is sometimes confidently wrong.

Part 1

What you are actually looking at

A generative AI tool — ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini — was built by reading an enormous amount of written text and learning which words tend to follow which other words. When you type a question, it does not go find an answer. It produces text, one piece at a time, that is statistically likely to follow what you wrote.

That is the whole mechanism.

It is a very good guess about what a well-informed answer to your question would look like. Most of the time a very good guess is correct, because it read a great deal of correct writing. But “looks like a correct answer” and “is a correct answer” are two different things, and the tool cannot tell them apart.

This is genuinely different from the other software you use. CARDS, Cap60, SmartForms and a spreadsheet all do the same thing every time because someone wrote a rule. An AI tool produces something new each time, and nobody wrote a rule for your specific question.

What it is

  • A writing and reading assistant
  • Fast at summarizing, drafting, reformatting and sorting
  • Good at turning dense policy into plain language
  • Tireless, and willing to try again

What it is not

  • A search engine — it is not looking anything up
  • A database — it stores no records
  • Connected to your systems — it cannot see CARDS, Cap60, your general ledger or your case files
  • Current — it may not know a rule that changed last month

That last column matters more than it first appears. The tool knows nothing about your agency unless a person types it in or pastes it in. That is why it needs a thorough briefing to be useful — the subject of Module 2. It is also the reason client privacy is a real risk: the only way your client data reaches an outside company's servers is if one of your staff puts it there.

Part 2

Why it sounds sure and is sometimes wrong

When an AI tool states something false, the industry calls it a hallucination. The word makes it sound like a rare glitch. It is not a glitch. It is the same process that produces correct answers, running on a question where the likely-sounding text happens not to be true.

Here is the part that catches people. The tool has no separate place where it checks whether it is right. Producing a fact and producing a plausible-sounding fabrication feel identical from the inside, because they are the same operation. So it delivers both in the same confident, well-organized, professional voice.

What it gives you “According to the most recent American Community Survey, the poverty rate in your county is 18.4 percent, with 22.1 percent of children under 18 living below the federal poverty line.”
What is true about it The format is right. The survey it names is real. The numbers are plausible. And they may be invented, because nothing in the tool went and looked them up.

Nothing in how that paragraph reads tells you which it is. You cannot catch a hallucination by noticing that it sounds uncertain, because it never does. You catch it by checking the claim against a real source.

The rule this gives you

Anything you would put your agency's name behind, you verify.

  • Numbers. Poverty rates, census figures, funding amounts, percentages. Check them against Census, SAIPE, your own FNPI file, or your own ledger.
  • Citations. It can produce a real-sounding report title, author and year that do not exist. If you cannot open it, do not cite it.
  • Program rules. Eligibility thresholds, income guidelines, allowable costs, deadlines. Check the regulation or the notice.
  • Anything about your own agency. It does not know your service area, your allocation, or how many households you served last quarter.

The practical version of this rule is simple: use AI where you can check the work. If you cannot tell whether an answer is right, you are not ready to use AI for that task yet. That is not a permanent limit — it is a signal about what to learn next.

Part 3

Assistant, not decider

This is the one distinction that governs everything in the rest of this course, and it is worth being precise about, because the two uses sound similar and are not.

Assistant It drafts, summarizes, reorganizes, translates, and checks. A person reads the output, fixes it, and signs their name to it. The person remains responsible.
Decider It scores, ranks, flags or recommends who gets served, who is at risk, who is eligible. A person may glance at the result, but the system shaped the outcome.

The evidence on these two uses is not equally strong, and the gap is large enough to plan around.

The case for assistive use
Nava Public Benefit Corporation, with Georgetown and Cornell, ran a randomized trial and a 14-week field pilot of an AI assistant that helped caseworkers navigate Medicaid and SNAP policy manuals. Caseworker accuracy improved by an average of 40 percent, with the largest gains on the hardest cases — exactly where a new or overloaded caseworker is most likely to err. The tool helped people find the rule. It never made the decision.
The case for caution
Allegheny County, Pennsylvania built a screening tool to help flag child-maltreatment risk. Because maltreatment is not directly measurable, the designers used the closest available stand-ins: re-referral and foster-care placement. It was a defensible choice, and it quietly built in who tends to get reported to authorities in the first place — and made families wealthy enough to use private services nearly invisible to the system. The tool was not malicious. It inherited the blind spots of the data it could see.
And when there is a person in the loop
“A human reviews it” is a real safeguard only when the reviewer can genuinely overrule the tool. Researchers document automation bias: people defer to a system's recommendation even when they have every right not to. A signature at the bottom of a screen is not oversight.

Why Community Action work sits on the assistive side

Your clients cannot take their business elsewhere. When a private company's algorithm gets something wrong, a customer switches products. When an agency's process gets something wrong, a household loses assistance it was entitled to, and often has no other place to go.

That asymmetry is the reason this course keeps AI pointed at paperwork rather than at people. The administrative burden is real — CSBG reporting consumes close to a full-time-equivalent staff year at agencies where it is the smallest grant they administer. That is a serious problem, and it is the right one to aim at.

Part 5

Where the rules live at your agency

A 2026 benchmark study of 346 nonprofits found that 92 percent were already using AI in some form, but only 7 percent reported any major gain in what their organization could do. The study attributed the gap to governance, not technology: 47 percent had no AI policy at all, and 81 percent used AI ad hoc, one person at a time, with no shared practice.

Which means the most likely situation at your agency is that several people are already using these tools and nobody has agreed on the rules. Finding out is a ten-minute task.

Before you finish this module

This one applies directly to you

You told us your agency works in CARDS. Worth being precise, then: an AI tool in a browser tab has no connection to CARDS whatsoever. Anything that moves between them moves because a person copied it. That is a limitation, and — as Module 3 covers — it is also the protection.

There is one more constraint specific to this network. In North Carolina, community service data-entry systems must be approved by the Office of Economic Opportunity. At least one agency — Mountain Projects — found new software with AI features that would have improved their referral tracking, and could not adopt it for that reason. So if the tool you are considering touches your case-management system rather than sitting alongside it, the question is not only “is this safe” but “is this permitted.” Ask before you buy.

Part 6

Quick self-check

Three questions. Pick an answer to see whether it holds up.

1. An AI tool gives you a confident, well-written answer about a CSBG income eligibility threshold. What is the most accurate thing to say about it?

Most of the time it will be close to right. The problem is that a correct answer and a fabricated one arrive looking exactly the same, so confidence carries no information. Check it against the regulation.

2. Which of these best describes what an AI tool is doing when it answers you?

It predicts likely text. There is no lookup step, no rulebook for your specific question, and no connection to your records — which is exactly why it needs a full briefing from you, and why it can invent a citation.

3. Your agency wants to use AI to help decide which households on the waitlist get served first. What is the strongest objection?

The research shows real gains when AI helps staff find information, and documented harm when it shapes decisions about people — because it inherits whatever bias is in the data it can see, and the people affected have no other agency to go to.

The five things that stick

  1. It predicts likely text. It is not searching, not looking anything up, and not consulting a rulebook written for your question.
  2. It cannot tell when it is wrong. A correct answer and an invented one are produced the same way and arrive in the same confident voice. Verify every number, citation and rule you put your agency's name behind.
  3. It never sees your systems. Nothing reaches it unless a person puts it there — which is both why it needs a thorough briefing and why client privacy is a live risk.
  4. Assistant, not decider. It drafts, summarizes and checks. A person decides and signs. The strongest evidence is for helping staff find information; the clearest harm is from systems that shape decisions about people.
  5. Ask before you paste. Know today whether your agency has an AI policy, whether your account trains on what you type, and who you go to when you are not sure.
