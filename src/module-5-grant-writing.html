Module 5 · AI for Grant Writing

You have written this before.

Almost every answer a new funder wants exists somewhere in an application your agency already submitted. The job is adaptation, not invention — and that is the thing these tools are genuinely good at.

Module 5 of six About 40 minutes Self-paced. Progress saved on this device.

What you will be able to do

  • Build a reusable library from your own past applications.
  • Adapt prior narrative to a new funder's questions without starting from a blank page.

Part 1

Why this is the safest use in the course

Grant writing has a property nothing else in this course has: there is no client data anywhere in it. A narrative describes your programs, your outcomes in aggregate, and your community. It is not client-facing, it does not touch a case record, and nobody's benefits depend on getting it right.

It also targets a gap agencies name themselves. Some agencies treat grant-writing as a survival skill and say so plainly — one executive director credited internal grant-writing capacity as the single thing that made her agency effective compared to a better-funded one she had worked at. Others run near-total dependence on CSBG with a single person doing everything. That difference is a structural equity problem: agencies with strong grant-writing capacity are more effective independent of how much need they face.

Across the national network, grant-writing talent has thinned for twenty years as skilled writers leave for better-paying employers, and positions sit vacant. Estimated current use of AI for grant writing is around one in ten of the more advanced agencies. There is a lot of room here.

Part 2

Build the library first

Do this once and every future application gets faster. Collect into one folder:

  • Every application you have submitted in the last three years — especially the funded ones, but keep the rejections too; they contain good language that lost for other reasons.
  • Program descriptions. The paragraph that explains what your weatherization program actually does, written well, once.
  • Outcome tables. Aggregate figures, with the year and source noted beside each one.
  • Your CNA summary. The needs data, already gathered and already paid for.
  • Boilerplate. Agency history, governance structure, service area, tripartite board composition, audit status.
  • Any reviewer feedback you have ever received. Rare and extremely valuable.

Everything on that list is agency-authored and aggregate

Which means the whole library is safe to work with. The one thing to watch: past applications sometimes contain a client success story with a real name. Strip those before the document goes anywhere — and check whether you had consent to use the story in the first place.

Part 3

Answering a new funder from old material

The core move. You have a question from a new application and a pile of things you have already written. You are asking for a translation, not a composition.

Copy this I'm the grants lead at a Community Action Agency in North Carolina. We're applying to a regional health foundation that funds work on social drivers of health. Their question is: "Describe how your organization addresses the root causes of health inequity in the communities you serve, and how you measure impact." Limit 600 words. Below is narrative from three of our past applications, plus our outcome table for the last two years. Draft an answer that: - uses our actual programs and actual numbers, nothing invented - leads with the connection between poverty and health outcomes, since that's this funder's framing - keeps our voice — plain, concrete, not promotional - marks with [CHECK] any place where you used a figure, so I can verify each one If our material doesn't actually answer part of their question, say so instead of filling the gap. I'd rather know we have a weakness than submit something that papers over it. [paste your past narrative and outcome table here]

If you named a funder at setup, swap that funder into the second paragraph and run it on a real question of theirs. Two instructions in there do most of the work. “Mark with [CHECK] any place you used a figure” gives you a verification checklist for free. “If our material doesn't answer part of their question, say so” stops it inventing a program you do not run — which it will otherwise do, fluently.

Part 4

Reviewer mode

The most underused technique in this module, and it costs nothing. Before you submit, have the tool read your draft the way the panel will.

Copy this Act as a grant reviewer scoring this application against the funder's stated criteria, which I've pasted below the narrative. You are reading forty of these today and you are tired. Tell me: 1. The weakest paragraph, and exactly why it's weak 2. Any claim I've made that I haven't backed with evidence 3. Any question a reviewer would have that this doesn't answer 4. Where I've said something generic that any agency could have written 5. What I'd need to add to move this from "fundable" to "funded" Be blunt. I'd rather hear it from you than from a rejection letter. [paste the funder's criteria, then your draft]

“You are reading forty of these today and you are tired” is not a joke. It produces noticeably harsher and more useful criticism than asking for a neutral review, because it matches the actual reading conditions.

Part 5

Removing the tells

Reviewers increasingly recognize machine-written prose, and it costs you credibility even when the underlying application is strong. The markers are consistent and easy to remove once you can see them.

The tellWhat it looks likeThe fix
Not just X, but Y “This program provides not just housing assistance, but a pathway to stability.” Pick one and say it. “This program keeps families housed while they rebuild income.”
Everything in threes Every list has exactly three items, every sentence has three clauses. Vary it. Two items. Five. One.
Vocabulary tells delve, leverage (as a verb), robust, landscape, tapestry, underscore, pivotal, holistic, testament to Say the plain word. “Use,” “strong,” “field.”
Uniform paragraphs Every paragraph is four sentences and roughly the same length. Let one be a single sentence. Let another run long.
Enthusiasm with no number “We are deeply committed to transformative impact for the families we serve.” “Last year 41 households left the program above the poverty line and 34 were still above it a year later.”
Hedged everything “may potentially help to support” “pays for.”

The fix is almost always the same move.

Replace a general claim with a figure from your own data. Machine-written prose is general because the machine does not know your numbers. You do. Every place you swap a confident abstraction for a specific local fact, the text stops sounding generated and starts sounding like the only agency that could have written it.

Part 6

Practice: rewrite this paragraph

Read it, find the tells, then see how many you caught.

The draft “Our organization is deeply committed to not just addressing immediate needs, but fostering long-term self-sufficiency across the vibrant tapestry of communities we serve. By leveraging robust partnerships, holistic case management, and transformative workforce development, we underscore our dedication to meaningful outcomes. Our comprehensive approach is a testament to the pivotal role community action plays in the anti-poverty landscape.”

Scan it and see

The paragraph is already loaded. Hit scan. Then delete it, paste something you have actually submitted, and scan that.

The count that matters is the second one. Markers are a symptom; the absence of numbers is the disease. Text with six figures in it and a couple of stray “robust”s reads as written by someone who knows the programme. Text with no figures and immaculate vocabulary reads as generated, because in a real sense it was — by something that did not have your data.

Eleven tells in three sentences: not-just-but, tapestry, vibrant, leveraging, robust, holistic, transformative, underscore, testament to, pivotal, landscape. And not a single number.

Rewritten “We served 148 households last year across four counties. Most came to us for rent or utility help. What we actually do is keep working with them after that bill is paid — on a job, a credential, a budget — for as long as two years. Last year seven families finished above the federal poverty line. That number is smaller than we want, and it is the number we are asking you to help us change.”

Shorter, specific, and it does something the original could not: it admits a weakness. Real numbers let you be honest, and honesty reads as credible to a reviewer who has spent all day being told about transformative impact.

Part 7

Quick self-check

1. The AI gives you a compelling paragraph citing a national statistic about rural poverty. What do you do?

Option C is the trap: it will happily produce a real-sounding report title, author and year that do not exist. If you cannot open the source yourself, the claim does not go in the application.

2. Which of these most reliably makes a narrative stop sounding machine-written?

Vocabulary swaps treat the symptom. The underlying cause is that the tool doesn't know your numbers, so it writes in generalities. Your data is the thing it cannot fake.

3. A past application contains a success story with a client's real first name and town. You're reusing it. What now?

Having submitted it before doesn't make it consented, and a first name plus a small town is identifying on its own. This is the one place client data sneaks into grant writing.

The five things that stick

  1. Start from what you have already written. The tool's job is adaptation, not invention — and invention is where it goes wrong.
  2. Build the library once. Past applications, program descriptions, outcome tables, CNA summary, boilerplate. Every future deadline gets shorter.
  3. Use reviewer mode before you submit. Ask for the weakest paragraph and the unanswered question. It is free criticism from the only critic who will read it twice.
  4. Every number and citation must trace to a document you hold. If you cannot open the source, cut the sentence.
  5. The cure for machine-sounding prose is your own data. Swap a general claim for a local figure and the problem mostly disappears.
