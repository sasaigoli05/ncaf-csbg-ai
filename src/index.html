One rule runs through the whole course

Aggregate data and your own words are fine. Anything that identifies a person is not. Every technique here works on the numbers you already totalled and the documents your agency already wrote. Nothing in this course ever asks you to put a client's name, address, date of birth or case record into an AI tool — and Module 3 explains exactly why that line sits where it does.

Why this exists

CSBG reporting consumes roughly a full-time-equivalent staff year at agencies where it is often the smallest grant they administer. Community Needs Assessments cost tens of thousands of dollars that small agencies do not have. Grant-writing capacity — the thing that most separates agencies that stretch their allocation from agencies that cannot — has thinned across the network for twenty years as skilled writers leave for better-paying employers.

Meanwhile, in interviews with twelve North Carolina Community Action Agencies, essentially none were conversant in AI. Not resistant — unfamiliar. The question was never whether agencies were willing. It was that nobody had shown them where to start, or where to stop.

Nationally, 92 percent of nonprofits report using AI in some form, and only 7 percent report any major gain in what their organization can do. Forty-seven percent have no AI policy at all. The gap is not the technology. It is that nobody agreed on the rules.

This course is the rules — written for this network, grounded in what agencies actually told us, and honest about the places where AI is the wrong answer. That last part matters as much as the rest.

How it is built

  • Tied to your actual work. Every example is a CSBG report, a needs assessment, a grant application or a client letter. No AI history, no theory you will not use on Monday.
  • Self-paced, in short pieces. Nobody running an agency has an eight-hour block. Each module is one sitting, and each part inside it stands alone.
  • Three to five takeaways at the end of every module. That recap is the part people keep.
  • Plain language. Written to an eighth-grade reading level wherever the subject allows it.
  • Something to do, not just read. Sorting exercises, self-checks, copy-ready prompts and worked examples in every module.

The format follows the National Community Action Foundation's “History of the War on Poverty” masterclass — the one training with a proven track record in this network. Directors completed it in roughly thirty-minute increments over about a month.
