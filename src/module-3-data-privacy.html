Module 3 · Data Privacy and Client Protection

You cannot take it back.

Most mistakes with an AI tool cost you a bad draft. This one is different. Client information pasted into an outside company's system is gone — there is no recall, no undo, and no way to know where it went. This is the module that matters most.

Module 3 of six About 35 minutes Self-paced. Progress saved on this device.

What you will be able to do

  • List the categories of client information that must never be entered into a general-purpose AI tool.
  • Explain why social-services client data is more sensitive than ordinary business data.
  • De-identify a record well enough to use it — and recognize when de-identification has failed.
  • Explain the difference between a consumer AI account and an organizational one, and say which your agency has.
  • Apply the aggregate-data-only standard to a task you actually perform.
Part 1

The never list

Start with the simple version. None of the following goes into ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini, a free tool you found online, or an AI feature inside a product your agency has not vetted — regardless of how careful you plan to be with the output.

Never

  • Names. Clients, household members, children, emergency contacts.
  • Addresses. Street address, and in a small county sometimes the road alone.
  • Social Security numbers. Ever, in any form, on any account.
  • Dates of birth. A date of birth plus a county is close to a unique identifier.
  • Phone numbers and email addresses.
  • Case, client, file or participant numbers. They link straight back to a person in your system.
  • Income detail tied to a household. Aggregate totals are fine; “this family earns $1,840 a month” is not.
  • Health, disability and behavioral health status.
  • Immigration-adjacent information. Status, country of origin, documentation questions.
  • Case narratives and intake notes. The richest and most dangerous record you hold.
  • Anything out of a Module 4C file. The All Characteristics Report is personal information by construction.

The list is long, but the test underneath it is short: could a reader work out which household this is? If the honest answer is “maybe, if they knew the area,” treat it as a yes.

Part 2

Why this data is different

Every organization says its data is sensitive. In a Community Action Agency the claim is literally true in a way it is not for a retailer, and it is worth being precise about why, because the reason shapes the rule.

The exposure is not embarrassment
A leaked marketing list produces spam. A leaked client file produces eligibility fraud committed in someone's name, identity theft against a household with no financial cushion to absorb it, and — where immigration-adjacent detail is involved — enforcement risk. These are not reputational harms. They are material ones, landing on people with the least capacity to recover.
Your clients cannot leave
A customer who dislikes a company's data practices switches products. A household that needs weatherization assistance in a county with one Community Action Agency has no second option. The relationship is not voluntary in the same way, which raises what the agency owes them — not lowers it.
You are holding it on trust, not by right
Clients disclosed income, household composition and health status because it was required to receive help, not because they chose to share it with you. That distinction is the whole ethical basis of the never list.
Part 3

The standard: aggregate data only

Here is the rule the whole course runs on, stated once, plainly.

Aggregate data and your own words are fine. Anything that identifies a person is not.

Aggregate data means counts and totals you have already rolled up — 142 households served, $38,400 spent in the Housing domain, 22 clients enrolled in employment programs. Your own words means documents your agency wrote: a needs assessment summary, a past grant narrative, a program description, a policy paragraph.

This is not a cautious compromise. It is where almost all of the available value actually sits. Run the CSBG Annual Report against the standard and the picture is clear:

Five of the seven pass. The two that fail cannot be made to pass by being careful — 4C works by matching personal identifiers, so there is no version of it that avoids them. Those stay inside the systems your agency already controls.

Part 4

When removing the name is not enough

De-identification is the skill that makes the rule usable. Done badly it produces false confidence, which is worse than no confidence at all.

The failure mode is re-identification: the text no longer contains a name, but the combination of details still points at one household. This is an acute risk in this network, because the counties are small. In a county of thirty thousand people there is often exactly one of whatever you just described.

Not de-identified “A 34-year-old single mother of three in Bryson City, living in a camper on her father's land after an eviction in March, whose youngest has a feeding tube.”
De-identified “A single parent with young children, recently evicted, currently in unstable housing, with a child who has significant medical needs.”

The second version keeps everything you need to draft a letter or plan a service response, and drops everything that points at a person. Notice what had to go: not just the name, but the specific age, the town, the month, the housing arrangement, and the particular medical detail. Any two of those together would have been enough.

Your turn: clean this up

A caseworker is about to paste this into an AI tool to get a follow-up letter drafted. Click everything that must come out first. There are eight.

Follow-up for Angela Rivers, case #44812, DOB 04/17/1988. Currently at 214 Sycamore Street in Bryson City. She is a single mother of two and received $487.50 in emergency utility assistance last month. Reachable at (828) 555-0174. She asked about the weatherization waitlist and mentioned her hours were cut at the plant.

Read back what is left once those eight are gone: a client received emergency utility assistance last month, asked about the weatherization waitlist, and has had their hours reduced. That is still enough to draft a useful letter from — and it identifies nobody. The information you actually needed survived. The information that put a household at risk did not.

Use this on real work

Redaction Check in the Toolkit does what you just did by hand, on anything you paste into it. It runs in your browser, so the text never leaves your computer — you can check that by turning off your wi-fi.

A working habit

Before you paste, read it back and ask: if a neighbour read this, would they know who it is? Not a stranger in another state — a neighbour. That is the right test for a county where the caseworker, the client and the landlord all shop at the same grocery store.

Part 5

Which account are you actually using?

Two people can type the same sentence into what looks like the same tool and get very different outcomes, because the account underneath is different. Most staff have no idea which they are on.

AccountWhat typically happens to what you typeUse it for
Free consumerOften used to improve the vendor's models. No agreement exists between the vendor and your agency.Nothing involving agency material you would not publish
Paid individualBetter, but the contract is with that staff member, not the agency. Leaves when they do.Personal productivity, not agency records
Organizational / businessCan usually be configured so inputs are not used for training, with a written data-processing agreement.Aggregate agency work, once configured

Worth raising this week

At setup you said staff are using free personal accounts. That is the row above with the weakest protection — free tiers commonly train on what users type, and no agreement exists between the vendor and your agency. This is not a reason to stop using AI. It is a reason to move to an organizational account before anyone pastes anything out of a real file.

Your first task after this module

At setup you were not sure what kind of AI account the agency has. Find out before you start Module 4 — it is the single fact that determines what is safe to type.

You are in the best position of the three

Your agency pays for accounts, which means the no-training setting is available to you. Available is not the same as switched on — that is the thing to go and verify.

“Not used for training” is the specific thing to ask for — often called a zero-retention or no-training setting. It is not automatic, and on most products it is off by default.

Find out this week

Two federal constraints worth knowing, because they come up: there is no unified per-person identifier across US benefits programs, and rules generally prohibit sharing client data across funding sources. Both mean the tidy cross-program matching an AI vendor may offer you is usually not something you are permitted to do in the first place.

Part 6

Practice: fix three of these

Each of these is something a staff member might reasonably type. Decide what is wrong before you reveal the answer.

One

As typed “Write a referral letter for James Whitaker, 412 Maple Road, who needs help with his power bill. He's behind $340 and his disability check comes on the 3rd.”

Name, street address, exact amount, benefit type and payment date. Five identifiers in two sentences. The fix is not to trim one — it is to describe the situation instead: “Write a referral letter template for a client seeking utility assistance who receives disability income and has a past-due balance. Leave blanks for name, address and amount.” You get a reusable template, and nothing left your building.

Two

As typed “Here's our intake spreadsheet for last quarter. Which clients are most likely to drop out of the program?”

Two separate problems, and the second is the worse one. The spreadsheet is client-level data. But even with perfect de-identification, the question asks the tool to make a prediction about specific people that would change how they are treated. That is the decider role from Module 1, in the setting where the evidence is weakest. Ask instead what the research says about common drop-out factors in case management, and apply your own judgment to your own caseload.

Three

As typed “Summarize these 40 client satisfaction survey responses into themes for our board report.”

This one is closer to fine than it looks, and the answer is “it depends on the responses.” Anonymous ratings and general comments are aggregate feedback. But open-text survey answers routinely contain names, staff names, and specific situations — “Angela helped me after my husband passed” identifies two people. Read them first, strip anything specific, then summarize. This is the most common near-miss in agency practice, because the task itself is entirely legitimate.

Part 7

Quick self-check

1. You removed every name from a case note before pasting it. Is that enough?

Age plus town plus household composition plus a distinctive circumstance can point at exactly one family in a small county. Removing the name is the first step, not the whole job.

2. Your agency wants AI help with Module 4C unduplicated counts. What should you say?

Unduplicated counting works by matching name, date of birth and address variants. Removing the identifiers removes the thing the task operates on. Keep it inside your own case-management system.

3. A colleague says “it's fine, I use the free version, we're not paying them anything.” What's the problem?

Not paying is exactly the issue. With no contract there is no commitment about retention, training or deletion, and nobody at your agency has agreed to any terms on the agency's behalf.

The five things that stick

  1. Aggregate data and your own words are fine. Anything that identifies a person is not. That one sentence covers almost every case you will meet.
  2. You cannot take it back. Unlike a bad draft, a paste is irreversible. Check before, not after.
  3. Removing the name is step one, not the whole job. Ask whether a neighbour would know who it is.
  4. Find out which account you are on. Free consumer tiers usually train on your input and carry no agreement with your agency.
  5. Some tasks cannot be made safe. Module 4C and case-level outcome tracking need personal identifiers by construction. They stay in your own systems.
