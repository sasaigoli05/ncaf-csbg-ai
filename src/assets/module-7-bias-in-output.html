Module 7 · Bias in What AI Writes for You

Read what it wrote about the person.

Most of the AI bias your agency will ever meet is not in a scoring system. It is in a case summary, a follow-up letter, a grant paragraph or a translated notice — text that looks finished and goes out under your name. This module is about catching it there.

Module 7 of seven About 35 minutes Self-paced. Progress saved on this device.

What you will be able to do

  • Spot deficit language in an AI draft and rewrite it into plain statements of fact.
  • Explain why changing only a name, a county or a language can change what an AI writes, and what to do about it.
  • Name the default assumptions AI makes about a household when you do not tell it otherwise.
  • Write a prompt that reduces biased framing and asks the AI to show its assumptions.
  • Run a short review on any AI-drafted text that describes a person or a community before it goes out.
Part 1

Where this bias actually shows up

Module 6 looked at tools that score people or make recommendations about them, and how to judge one before you adopt it. Most agencies in this network will never buy a tool like that. Nearly all of them already use AI to draft.

That is where bias usually enters your work. Not as a decision, but as a sentence. A case summary that calls someone “noncompliant.” A letter that is warmer to one client than another. A grant narrative that describes your community as a list of problems.

It writes about people the way people are most often written about.

You saw in Module 1 that these tools predict likely text. So when you ask one to describe a family on energy assistance, it reaches for the way such families are usually described. Much of what it learned from — news coverage, forms, old reports, online comments — does not describe them kindly or accurately.

Three things make this easy to miss. The text is fluent, so it reads as finished. The bias is in word choice, not in facts, so a fact check will not catch it. And the words that do the damage are often ones you have heard at work for years.

Why it matters more than it looks

  • Case notes follow a person. The next caseworker reads “unmotivated” before they meet her.
  • Clients read letters. A cold or suspicious tone tells someone how your agency sees them.
  • Grant narratives shape the picture funders hold of your agency's community for years.
Part 2

Deficit framing

Deficit framing describes people by what they lack instead of what is happening to them and what they want. AI does it by default, because so much of what it learned from does too.

The fix is not a list of banned words. Eligibility terms are facts, and funders need them. “Household income is below 150 percent of the poverty guidelines” is fine. The problem is the layer an AI adds on top of the facts: judgments, predictions and adjectives nobody in the room said.

What the AI wrote “Client unfortunately lacks reliable transportation and has struggled to maintain stable employment.”
What the notes actually said “Car is in the shop until Friday. Changed jobs twice this year, both times for more hours.”

The second version is shorter, more useful, and true. It also tells the next caseworker what to do. The first one only tells them what to think.

Your turn: find what the AI added

A caseworker gave an AI tool her de-identified notes and asked for a case summary. Click every phrase that judges, predicts or assumes something the notes do not say. There are seven. The plain facts are fine.

Case summary, energy assistance follow-up. Client is a struggling single mother with two children, ages 6 and 9. Household income is below 150 percent of the federal poverty guidelines. She unfortunately lacks reliable transportation and admitted that she fell behind on the electric bill in January after her hours were cut. Despite her circumstances, she brought all required documents to the appointment. Client works part-time at a grocery store. She will likely need ongoing support to manage her household budget. Client appears to have limited understanding of the application process. Next step: submit crisis assistance application by Friday. Client is at risk of becoming dependent on assistance.

A quick test for any sentence about a person

Could you point to the moment in the notes where this happened, or where the person said it? If yes, keep it, in plain words. If it is an adjective, a guess about the future, or a word like unfortunately, admitted, despite or noncompliant, cut it or replace it with the fact underneath.

Part 3

Same request, different name

Here is the uncomfortable part. Give an AI tool the same request twice, change only the client's name, and you can get a different letter back. Sometimes a different tone. Sometimes different options. Sometimes a warning the first client did not get.

This has been tested. A 2024 audit from Stanford researchers asked several AI models for advice about people in the same situations, changing only the name. The advice varied systematically with names associated with race and gender, and names associated with Black women tended to get the least favourable results. Journalists found a similar pattern the same year when they had an AI model rank résumés that were identical except for the name.

The model is not deciding to treat anyone differently.

It is predicting likely text, and a name, a county or a language changes what “likely” means. That is exactly why it is hard to see. Each letter looks fine on its own. You only notice when you put two side by side.

Put two side by side

Pick a comparison. Each one sends the same request with one detail changed. Read both replies, then judge each difference: does it matter, or is it fine? Finish one comparison to continue. The others show different patterns if you want them.

These replies were written by the Task Force to show patterns that audits have described. They are not captured from a specific tool, and any given tool on any given day may do better or worse. All names are made up.

Hi Emily,

Thanks for applying for energy assistance. We just need a copy of your most recent utility bill to finish your application. You can email a photo of it, drop it off at our office, or mail it to us.

Let us know if you have any questions!

Dear Keisha Robinson,

Your application for energy assistance is incomplete. You must submit a copy of your most recent utility bill. Applications without all required documents may be denied.

If you need help understanding this process, a caseworker can assist you.

The first note is warm and thanks her. The second opens by calling her application incomplete.
The first note lists three ways to send the bill. The second lists none.
Only the second note mentions denial and offers help understanding the process.
One note ends with an exclamation mark and the other does not.

Hello,

To finish your energy assistance application, please upload a copy of your most recent utility bill through our online portal, or email us a photo of it.

Thank you!

Hello,

To finish your energy assistance application, we need a copy of your most recent utility bill. If you don't have internet access, you can mail it to us, or ask a family member to drive it to our office. We understand getting into town can be hard.

Thank you!

The Graham County note assumes the client has no internet. The Wake County note assumes they do.
The Wake County note offers only online options. The Graham County note offers only offline ones.
The Graham County note suggests a family member drive the bill over.
Both notes open with “Hello” and end with “Thank you!”

Hello,

We're almost done with your energy assistance application. We just need a copy of your most recent utility bill. You can email a photo, drop it off, or mail it to us by Friday.

Thanks, and call us with any questions.

Hello,

We need your utility bill. Please bring the bill to our office. Bring it by Friday.

If you need help, please bring a family member who speaks English.

The note for the Spanish-speaking client is written in English.
It tells her to bring a family member who speaks English.
Her note uses short, choppy sentences and gives only one way to send the bill.
Both notes give the same Friday deadline.

What to do about it

  • Leave the name out when it is not needed. You are already doing this for privacy (Module 3). It removes the signal too. Add the name yourself at the end.
  • Set the tone and the options in the prompt. “Warm, three sentences, list all three ways to send documents.” Then every client gets the same letter.
  • When a detail is needed, like language, say what it should change and what it should not. “Write it in Spanish. Keep everything else identical to the English version.”
  • Spot check. Once in a while, run the same request twice with only one detail changed. It takes two minutes and it is the only way to see this.
Part 4

What it assumes when you don't say

An AI tool never leaves a gap empty. If you do not tell it about the household, it fills in a typical one. And the typical household in its training is not the household that walks into a Community Action office.

What it fills in without asking

  • A household. Two parents, or a single mother. Rarely a grandparent raising grandchildren, adult siblings, or a man on his own with kids.
  • Access. A car, home internet, an email address, a printer, a phone with minutes left.
  • A schedule. A weekday job with a lunch break, so “call us between 9 and 5” seems easy.
  • A language and a reading level. English, read fluently, with forms that feel familiar.
  • A paper trail. A lease, pay stubs, a bank account. Many clients have some of these and not others.

None of these assumptions is malicious. Each one quietly shapes the draft. A letter that says “log in to the portal to upload your documents” works for some clients and fails others without anyone noticing.

For Head Start family communications

Watch for “parents” and “mom and dad” in AI drafts to families. Many children in your classrooms live with grandparents, foster parents, or one parent. “Families” or “caregivers” fits everyone.

Tell it what you know, and tell it not to guess

The fix is the one you learned in Module 2: brief it like a new coworker. Give it the facts you have. Tell it not to fill in the rest. Then ask it to show you what it assumed, so you can check. Copy this and add it to the end of any request that describes a person or a community.

Add this to any request that describes a person Describe people by what happened and what they want, not by what they lack. Use only facts I have given you. Do not add adjectives about the person's character, motivation or ability, and do not predict what they will need in future. Do not assume anything about their household, transportation, internet access, work schedule, language or reading level. If something matters and I have not told you, say so instead of guessing. After the draft, list every assumption you made, so I can check it.

That last line is the most useful one. The list it gives back is often where you find the problem, because the assumption is now written down where you can see it.

Part 5

Translation and “make it simpler”

AI translation is fast and often good. It is also where small errors become large ones, because the person reading it usually cannot check it, and the person sending it usually cannot either.

Program terms
Words like weatherization, income-eligible or crisis assistance often have no clean equivalent. The AI picks something close, and close can mean a different program.
Deadlines, amounts and rights
A date format flipped, a dollar amount rounded, “may” turned into “must.” These are the errors that cost a client their benefit or their appeal.
Register
Translations can come out stiff and bureaucratic, or overly casual. Either one can read as disrespectful to the person receiving it.

Agencies that receive federal funds have obligations to provide meaningful access to people with limited English. An unchecked machine translation of a notice about someone's rights is a weak way to meet that. Ask whoever handles compliance at your agency what your language access plan says.

Treat AI translation as a first draft.

Before a translated message goes out, a fluent speaker checks three things: every deadline, every dollar amount, and every sentence about what the person is allowed to do or owed. If you have no fluent speaker on staff, that is a partner question, not a reason to skip the check.

“Simplify this” can talk down

Plain language is good practice for everyone. But ask an AI to “make this simpler for a low-income client” and you are telling it who it is writing for, and it will guess at what that means. You saw the result in the language comparison in Part 3: shorter sentences, fewer options, a tone like talking to a child.

Asks it to judge the reader “Make this simpler for a low-income client.”
Describes the text you want “Rewrite this in plain language: short sentences, common words, and keep every option and deadline.”

The second prompt describes the writing, not the reader. Use it for every letter, not just some.

Part 6

Rewrite it, then own it

Here is a sentence of the kind AI produces when asked for a grant needs statement. Every word of it is common in real applications. That is the problem.

Rewrite this sentence

Keep what a funder needs to know. Drop what judges the people you serve. Two or three sentences is fine.

“Our clients are low-income families trapped in a cycle of poverty who lack the skills and motivation to improve their situation.”
One way to rewrite it “Families come to our agency after a job loss, a cut in hours or a medical bill. Most tell us they want steadier, better-paid work, and [number] enrolled in our job training program last year.”

Put your own figure in the brackets. Notice that nothing a funder needs was lost. What went was “trapped,” “lack the skills” and “motivation,” none of which anyone measured.

Check your version

The honest limit

Everything in this module reduces bias in what AI writes for you. Nothing in it removes bias. A better prompt gets you a better draft. It does not get you a neutral one, and no setting, tool or vendor will.

That is why the last step is always a person. The staff member who sends the letter or signs the report owns every word in it, including the words the AI chose. “The AI wrote that” is not something you can say to a client.

Part 7

Quick self-check

1. An AI case summary says a client is “unfortunately unable to maintain stable employment.” Your notes say she changed jobs twice this year, both times for more hours. What is the best fix?

The job changes are useful information for the next caseworker, so deleting loses something. Softening keeps the false framing and adds a new judgment. The fact is shorter, true, and tells the reader what actually happened.

2. You ask for the same follow-up letter twice, changing only the client's name. The second letter is noticeably colder. What should you do?

Output does vary, but a pattern tied to a name is not random noise. You do not have to stop using the tool. Take the name out, specify the tone and options yourself, and review every letter the same way, so every client gets the same one.

3. A Spanish-speaking family needs a notice about an enrollment deadline. You translate it with AI. What happens next?

AI translation is a good first draft. The errors that cost people benefits are in dates, amounts and rights, and the person receiving it cannot check them. A fluent speaker can, in a few minutes.

The five things that stick

  1. AI writes about people the way people are most often written about. Most often, that is not kindly or accurately.
  2. Describe what happened, not what someone is. Facts survive the rewrite. Labels, predictions and “unfortunately” do not.
  3. If a name, county or language is not needed, leave it out. If it is needed, say what it should change and what it should not.
  4. Tell it what you know and tell it not to guess. Then ask it to list its assumptions, and read the list.
  5. You send it, you own it. Prompting reduces bias. Only a person reading carefully catches what is left.
