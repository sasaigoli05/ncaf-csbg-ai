NCAF AI Task Force · Governance

Seven guardrails, and the thing that does each one

These are the minimum governance steps the Task Force recommends a Community Action Agency put in place before deploying AI tools. Each one below says who does it, why it is here, and which part of this site actually carries it out — because a recommendation nobody can action is just a document.

Why a CAA-sized standard exists at all

There is no comprehensive federal law on organizational AI use. North Carolina does have a Responsible Use of Artificial Intelligence Framework for public agencies, updated August 2025, which requires them to keep an inventory of AI tools and to assess risk using the NIST framework before deployment.

It does not apply to you. The state framework covers state agencies, and roughly 80 percent of Community Action Agencies are private nonprofits. So most CAAs sit outside it entirely — while holding the same sensitive client records and reporting into the same state and federal systems.

That gap is the reason for this page. The full NIST framework was built over a multi-year process with hundreds of organizations, and not one of them had three staff and no IT department. What follows is the subset that survives contact with an agency that size.

The seven

Agency-level
1

Keep an inventory of the AI tools in use

Record each tool's name, what it is used for, who is responsible for it, and what client data it touches. Without this, leadership has no record of which tools are in use or for what — and in practice individual staff end up using personal accounts for case notes, grant drafts and report figures.

2

Name one AI-accountable person

One named staff member — usually the executive director — signs off on any AI-assisted output before it is final. That matters most for CSBG Annual Report and ROMA/NPI figures, where a number has to be defensible in review. Accountability spread across everyone is accountability nobody holds.

3

Assign someone to track changes and keep policy current

Tools, terms of service and state guidance all move. One person should be the contact point for questions and should keep the agency's position up to date. This can be the same person as guardrail 2 at a small agency — the point is that it is somebody's job rather than nobody's.

Future of Privacy Forum, Internal Policy Checklist

4

Update the employee handbook to cover generative AI

Most agencies already have a confidentiality policy that forbids disclosing client information to outside parties. An AI vendor is an outside party. Say so explicitly, decide whether staff must use organizational accounts rather than personal ones, and write down what may never be entered.

Future of Privacy Forum, Internal Policy Checklist

5

Train staff on the limits of AI output

Inaccuracy, bias, confidentiality, data minimisation, and the fact that the person who submits the work is responsible for it regardless of what produced the draft. Verification matters most wherever accuracy has to be certified — which in this sector means the financial and performance figures that go to a funder.

FPF checklist · NIST AI RMF Govern 2.2

6

Restrict confidential client data through settings and permissions

Not only through instruction. Check the account type, switch off training on your inputs where the setting exists, and control who can reach what. One concrete thing to check today: CARDS reports export to Excel with client and caseworker names included unless staff remove them, and those exports paste into a consumer AI tool as easily as anything else.

FPF checklist · NC framework, privacy by default

7

Keep a simple log of AI errors and corrections

When something comes out wrong, write down what it was, how it was caught and what changed. Two reasons: your own agency learns faster than it would from memory, and pattern data across agencies is the only way anyone spots a tool failing systematically rather than once.

Task Force agency-level recommendation 7

One rule worth memorising

Aggregate, never infer.

AI may aggregate and pre-populate data your agency already collected. It may not infer or fill in what is missing. Missing data gets flagged, not estimated.

This is proposed as a rule for the state data system, and it works just as well as a personal one. An estimate that reaches a federal report is indistinguishable from a count once it is in the cell — and a lower number you can defend beats a fuller one you cannot.

What this is drawn from

The guardrails come from the Task Force's agency-level policy recommendations, which adapt three sources: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, the North Carolina State Government Responsible Use of Artificial Intelligence Framework, and the Future of Privacy Forum's Internal Policy Checklist. Each provision was screened against a single question — could an agency with three to five CSBG staff and no IT capacity actually do this? Anything needing specialist staff, new spending or continuous monitoring was cut.

The underlying brief is still in draft and carries open citations. See the Library for its status.
