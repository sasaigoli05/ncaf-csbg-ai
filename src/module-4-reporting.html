Module 4 · AI for CSBG Reporting and Compliance

Using AI for CSBG reporting, without sending errors to a funder

Reporting takes real hours, and much of it is repetitive. AI can take some of that weight off. It cannot certify a number, and it should never be the final signer on a compliance report. This module shows where AI helps, and how to check its work before anything reaches a funder.

Module 4 of six About 30 minutes Self-paced. Progress saved on this device.

What you will be able to do

  • Map your CSBG reporting workflow and mark the steps where AI could cut manual effort.
  • Use AI to draft narrative sections of a report from de-identified summary data.
  • Use AI to flag obvious errors and inconsistencies in a dataset before submission.
  • Check an AI-drafted section against the source data for made-up facts, wrong numbers, and overstated claims.
  • Identify which reporting steps must stay fully human, and explain why.
First

Words used in this module

CSBGCommunity Services Block Grant. The federal funding that supports this work. It is administered by the HHS Office of Community Services (OCS).
ROMAResults Oriented Management and Accountability. The framework CAAs use to track and report outcomes.
National Performance IndicatorsStandard measures in the CSBG report that show what results the funding produced.
PIIPersonally identifiable information. Any detail that can identify a specific person, such as a name, address, birth date, or Social Security number. Small details can also combine to identify someone.
De-identificationRemoving or masking details so data cannot be traced back to a specific person.
Part 1

Map your workflow before you add AI

You cannot decide where AI helps until you can see the whole reporting process laid out. Break the report into steps. For each step, note who does it, where the data comes from, and how long it takes. Only then mark the slow, repetitive steps where a tool might help.

The repetitive steps are the candidates. The judgment steps are not.

A data coordinator lists her quarterly steps: pull service counts from the case system, clean the spreadsheet, calculate outcome rates, write the narrative, and check totals. The slow parts are cleaning the spreadsheet and drafting the narrative. Those are where a tool might help. Calculating the official outcome rate is not, because that number goes straight into the report.

Part 2

Where AI helps, and where it must not

What AI can help with

  • Draft a narrative section from summary numbers you provide
  • Reword a rough paragraph into plain language
  • Reformat notes into a consistent structure
  • Scan a dataset for obvious errors, like blank fields, duplicate rows, or a percentage above 100

What AI should not do

  • Produce or certify the official numbers
  • Decide what counts toward an indicator
  • See raw client PII
  • Be the final signer on a compliance report

AI is useful for first drafts and for spotting things a tired person misses. A reporting lead pastes a de-identified summary table into an AI tool and asks it to draft two paragraphs describing the quarter. She also asks it to list any rows where the numbers look inconsistent. She now has a starting point and two things to double-check.

The wrong job for AI

An agency is tempted to let AI “calculate our placement rate from the case notes.” That is the wrong job for AI. The rate is an official figure, the case notes contain PII, and what counts as a placement is a judgment call tied to ROMA. Staff calculate the rate themselves from de-identified counts, using the agency's agreed definition.

Part 3

Feed it only de-identified summaries

Before any data goes into an AI tool, strip it down to counts and summaries with no PII. The tool should see “240 households received energy assistance,” not a list of names, addresses, or case IDs. If you cannot remove enough detail to make a person untraceable, the data does not go in the tool.

Do not paste this A client roster with names, addresses, dates of birth, and case notes.
Paste this instead A summary table: service type and the number of households or individuals for each. No names. No addresses. No dates of birth.

Stop and check with a human

Before any data enters an AI tool, confirm the input is de-identified summary data. No names, no addresses, no case IDs, no raw client records. If you cannot make a person untraceable, the data does not go in the tool.

Part 4

Always verify the output

AI writes confidently even when it is wrong. It can invent a number, change a real number, or state a claim the data does not support. This is often called a made-up fact, or a hallucination. Before you use any AI-drafted section, check every number against the source, cut anything the data does not support, and watch for framing that overstates results or leans on a stereotype about the people served.

For example, an AI draft says the agency “served a record number of families.” The data does not show that. It is a comparison no one checked. Staff cut the claim and keep only what the numbers support.

Stop and check with a human

Before any number enters the submitted report, a person verifies each figure against the source data. AI does not certify numbers. A number that appears only in an AI draft is not confirmed until a human traces it to the source.

Stop and check with a human

Before the report is submitted, a named person signs off that they reviewed every AI-assisted section against the source. AI output is never submission-ready on its own.

Part 5

Practice: catch the errors before submission

This exercise uses made-up data only. Rivertown Community Action is a fictional agency. All numbers are invented for practice. Here is the true data for the quarter.

MeasureCount
Households served (Q2)412
Individuals served (Q2)1,050
Households receiving energy assistance240
Individuals receiving a job training referral96
Individuals who secured employment after training38
Households receiving food assistance310

Here is what the AI drafted from that table

Read it one sentence at a time and trace each number and claim back to the table. Click anything that does not hold up. There are four.

“During Q2, Rivertown Community Action served 412 households, representing 1,500 individuals. Energy assistance reached 240 households. Our job training program achieved a 60% placement rate, and 96 individuals secured full-time employment. Food assistance was the agency’s most-used service.”

What the four have in common

One changed number, one wrong calculation, one output reported as an outcome, and one comparison with nothing behind it. None of them looks wrong. Every one of them reads as competent, specific, professional writing — which is exactly why the check has to be a routine rather than a feeling.

Part 6

Quick self-check

Three questions. Pick an answer to see whether it holds up.

1. Who should certify the official numbers that go into a CSBG report?

Certification is an accountability act, not a clerical one. A number that exists only in an AI draft is unconfirmed until a person has traced it back to the source.

2. You want AI to help draft a report section. What can you safely paste in?

Deleting afterward changes nothing — the data has already left your building. Counts and summaries carry everything a draft needs and identify nobody.

3. An AI draft says your agency “served a record number of families.” Your data does not show any comparison to past years. What do you do?

Asking the tool to check itself is the trap — it has no separate verification step and will confirm its own claim. If the source does not show it, it does not go in.

The five things that stick

  1. Map your workflow first. Hand AI the repetitive steps, not the judgment steps.
  2. AI drafts and flags. People decide, certify, and sign off.
  3. Only de-identified summaries go into the tool. Never raw client records.
  4. Trace every number back to the source before you trust it.
  5. No AI-assisted section is submission-ready until a named person has checked it.
For facilitators and editors — not part of the learner module

Likely staff anxieties

“What if AI sends an error to our funder?” This is why verification is the core skill here, not an add-on. AI drafts and flags, but people check and certify. The AI Output Check catches errors more reliably than the all-manual process, where a tired person rereads their own work. Present the checklist as protection for the agency.

“Will this replace my job?” Point to the Assist Map. Every row that matters still ends in a human sign-off. AI takes the repetitive parts. The judgment, the definitions, the funder relationships, and the accountability stay with staff. The tool does the typing; the person does the deciding. If staff are new to AI, do the Part 5 exercise together on a screen before anyone tries it alone.

Verify before this reaches real agencies

An AI ethics or data-privacy expert should confirm: the de-identification standard and which privacy law applies to the agency's data (HIPAA, state law, or funder rules, which vary); the CSBG report fields, ROMA indicators, and NPI definitions against current OCS or state guidance; and whether the specific AI tool staff use has a data agreement that permits even de-identified agency data. All figures and the agency “Rivertown Community Action” are synthetic.
