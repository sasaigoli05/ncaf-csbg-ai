Prompting is briefing a new coworker

The AI is fast and knows a lot in general. It knows nothing about your agency, your clients, or how you like things done. This module shows you how to brief it so it gives you work you can actually use.

Part 1

The one idea to remember

Think about the last time a capable new hire started at your agency. They were smart and willing, but on day one they did not know what CSBG stands for, who your clients are, or that your board likes reports kept to one page. You had to tell them. The quality of their work depended on how well you explained the job.

An AI tool is that new coworker, every single time.

It will not ask you for the background it is missing. It will just fill the gaps with guesses. Your job is to give it the briefing a new coworker would need. Everything else in this module is a way to do that better.

Part 2

Three habits of a good briefing

1. Give context

Tell it who you are, what your agency does, what you are working on, and who the result is for. The same task changes completely depending on the audience.

Weak "Write a summary of this report."
Stronger "I'm the director of a Community Action Agency in North Carolina. Summarize this CSBG quarterly report for our board, who are community volunteers with no background in the program. One page, plain language, most important points first."

The second version tells it the audience, the length, and the tone. That is what makes the result usable.

2. Be specific, and set standing instructions

Say exactly what you want: length, format, reading level, tone. Then use a feature most tools now have. You can save instructions the tool applies to every response, so you stop repeating yourself. In Claude these are called Projects or custom instructions. In ChatGPT they are called custom instructions. You set them once.

Example standing instruction to save once For every response: write at about an 8th-grade reading level, never include client names or personal details, use short section headers, and keep paragraphs to four sentences or fewer.

3. Build on the conversation

Treat the first answer as a draft, not the final version. Tell it what to change. You can also ask it to explain how it reached an answer, which lets you check the reasoning instead of trusting it.

Example follow-up This draft is too formal for a client letter. Rewrite it in a warmer, simpler tone. Then list which facts you took from the notes I gave you, so I can check them.
Part 3

The kinds of tasks it is good for

Most useful agency prompts fall into one of these six jobs. Each one shows up in daily work.

Extract and summarizePull the key numbers from a long report. Summarize a 40-page funding notice into a page.
DraftA first draft of a grant narrative, a client letter, or a board memo.
Rewrite and simplifyTurn dense policy or an eligibility paragraph into plain language at a lower reading level.
Reorganize and formatTurn messy meeting notes into a clean table. Reformat data for a report.
BrainstormList supplemental grants worth pursuing. Outline questions for a Community Needs Assessment.
Check and critique"What questions might a reviewer ask about this application?" "What is missing here?"

Here is a real one you can adapt for grant work.

Ready-to-use grant prompt I'm applying for a supplemental grant to expand our weatherization program. Here is the funder's priorities section: [paste it]. Write three sentences connecting our program to each priority. Do not invent any numbers. Leave a blank where I need to add our own data.

Six ways to phrase a prompt

Those are the jobs. There is also a set of ways to phrase the request itself. MIT Sloan's guide to effective prompts groups them into a few common patterns. Here they are in plain terms, each with an agency example.

Just ask (zero-shot). Give clear instructions with no examples. Best for quick, general requests. "Summarize this intake policy in five bullet points."
Show examples first (few-shot). Paste one or two samples so it matches your format or tone. "Here are two past board summaries we liked. Write the next one in the same style."
Start with a verb (instructional). Lead with a direct command like write, explain, compare, or list. "Write a 100-word executive summary of this memo."
Give it a role (role-based). Ask it to take on a viewpoint. Useful for review and specialized work. "Act as a grant reviewer. Point out the weakest part of this narrative."
Set the scene (contextual). Add background and audience before the ask. "This is for clients reading at a 6th-grade level. Rewrite this eligibility section in simpler words."
Behind-the-scenes rules (meta or system prompt). Instructions that set how the AI behaves before you type anything. You do not usually write these directly, but the standing instructions from Part 2 are the version you control. "Always respond in plain language and never guess at a fact."

Adapted from MIT Sloan Teaching & Learning Technologies, Effective Prompts for AI: The Essentials.

Part 4

What it does not do well

Knowing the limits is part of using it responsibly. A good briefing cannot fix these.

  • It does not know your data. It has no access to your case management system, your latest CSBG numbers, or last week's board meeting unless you give it.
  • It is not current. It may not know a recent legislative change, such as a CSBG reauthorization update, unless you paste it in.
  • It cannot check its own facts. It can sound completely confident and still be wrong.
  • It does not know your community. It is a drafting aid, not a decision-maker.

Flaws and risks to watch

  • It makes things up. It can invent a statistic, a citation, or a program rule. Verify any number, quote, or fact before you report or submit it.
  • Client privacy. Never enter names, addresses, Social Security numbers, income details, or case notes into a public AI tool. Remove or replace personal details first, or use only a tool your agency has approved and secured.
  • Bias. AI can reflect bias from its training data. One widely cited case involved an image tool lightening a person's skin and changing their features when asked for a professional headshot. This matters most for work that touches who your community is or who gets served, such as a Community Needs Assessment. Keep a person reviewing that work.
  • Over-reliance. If you cannot check whether an answer is right, you are not ready to use the tool for that task.

Focus on the problem, not just the prompt

Tools are getting better at fixing rough prompts on their own. That means the lasting skill is not memorizing prompt tricks. It is defining the problem clearly: what you need, who it is for, and what a good result looks like. If you can state the problem well, the wording takes care of itself.

This point draws on MIT Sloan's summary of work by Oguz Acar.

Part 5

Warm-up: fix a weak prompt

Here is a request a coworker might type. Read it, then think about what a new coworker would still need to know before they could do the job.

The prompt "Write a grant summary."

What is missing? Think about the three habits: context, specifics, and audience. When you have an answer in your head, reveal a stronger version.

Stronger "I'm the grants manager at a Community Action Agency. I need a summary of our completed weatherization grant for our annual report. The readers are county commissioners. Keep it to 150 words, plain language, and lead with the number of households served. Here is the full report: [paste it]. Do not add numbers that are not in the report."

It names the role, the purpose, the audience, the length, the lead point, and a rule against inventing data. That is a briefing a new coworker could actually run with.

Part 6

Quick self-check

Three questions. Pick an answer to see whether it holds up.

1. You need the AI to summarize a report for your board. What is the single most useful thing to add to your request?

The tool writes very differently for a volunteer board than for a program officer. Telling it the audience and length is what makes the summary usable.

2. The AI gives you a poverty statistic for your county to use in a grant application. What should you do?

AI can state made-up numbers with full confidence. Anything you submit or report has to be checked against a real source.

3. A caseworker wants to use a public AI tool to draft a follow-up letter to a client. What has to happen first?

Client personal information should never go into a public AI tool. Strip identifying details first, or use only an approved, secured tool.
