What agencies told us

Nine themes, raised independently across interviews

The funding formula is universally seen as broken

Every agency raised it unprompted. No cost-of-living adjustment, built on poverty counts believed to undercount rural and low-income households, and blind to national forest land, tribal land and second-home populations. Essentially unchanged since 1981.

Administrative burden is disproportionate to award size

Agencies describe CSBG as their smallest grant and their most labour-intensive report. Requirements accumulated over time — organizational standards, smart forms, quarterly and monthly cycles — each reasonable alone, the total out of proportion.

Outcome tracking after exit is near-nonexistent

CSBG has no federal exit-tracking requirement, and the state data system has no follow-up function. Most agencies learn what happened only if a client volunteers it — which makes them uncompetitive for the supplemental grants they depend on.

Transportation is a top barrier, and almost nobody has data on it

Named by nearly every agency. One agency flagged that no transportation dataset exists to request at all — a statewide measurement gap rather than an agency oversight.

Grant-writing capacity decides who thrives

Agencies that treat leveraging outside funds as a survival skill outperform those with a single point of failure, independent of the need they face. That is a structural equity problem, and the network's grant-writing talent has thinned for twenty years.

Needs assessments miss what residents cannot name

One agency found its prior assessment had oversampled respondents above the poverty line. Another discovered an urgent connectivity crisis that was absent from the previous assessment entirely — because nobody had thought to ask.

State approval can block better tools

Community service data-entry systems require state approval. At least one agency identified software with AI features that would have improved referral tracking and had to abandon it for that reason. Policy, not technology, is the binding constraint.

Baseline AI literacy is very low

Across the agencies interviewed, essentially none were conversant in AI. Not resistant — unfamiliar. The question was never willingness; it was that nobody had shown them where to start or where to stop.

One agency shows what is possible

A single agency in the set runs a dedicated data analyst, custom dashboards, social vulnerability index mapping and closed-loop digital referrals. It is the outlier, and it is the benchmark for what the rest could reach with support.

What the evidence says about AI here

Six themes from the literature review

The value is administrative, not autonomous

Across every review examined, the demonstrated benefit of AI in social services is administrative and decision-support — reduced bureaucratic burden, faster document review. Evidence for improved decision accuracy is far weaker and mostly anticipated rather than shown.

Governance is the constraint, not tooling

The binding limit on small organisations is capacity and governance, not access to tools. The 92%-using / 7%-benefiting gap is attributed directly to governance failure: no policy, ad hoc individual use, no shared workflow.

Staff-facing beats client-facing on evidence

The strongest result in the field is a randomised trial of a tool that helped caseworkers find the right rule. The most common real-world deployment is a client-facing chatbot. The gap in evidentiary rigour between the two is itself a caution.

Proxies are where harm enters

The best-documented failure in this field came from a defensible choice of stand-in measures for something unmeasurable — which encoded who gets reported and made better-resourced families invisible. That translation is a policy decision, however technical it looks.

Early adoption does not sustain itself

In the strongest pilot on record, tool usage declined over time without deliberate organisational effort. Any adoption plan that stops at training is a plan for a ninety-day result.

Nobody has studied this network

No source examines AI adoption within the CSBG network specifically. Every piece of evidence is borrowed from an adjacent setting. That gap is this project's opportunity as much as its limitation.

About the Task Force

The NCAF AI Task Force is a student-led project at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, working with the National Community Action Foundation, the North Carolina Community Action Association, and the NC DHHS Office of Economic Opportunity. Its purpose is to identify, teach and share responsible AI tools and guidance that measurably and safely reduce administrative burden on Community Action Agencies.
