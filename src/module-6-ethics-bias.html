Part 1

Three kinds of bias

The word “bias” covers three different failures, and they need different fixes. NIST separates them, and each one has a Community Action example close to hand.

Systemic
Built into the data, the process or the institution before any algorithm arrives. One NC agency discovered its own prior Community Needs Assessment had oversampled respondents well above the poverty guidelines — the opposite of what the assessment was for. No AI involved. The bias was in who answered the survey. An AI trained on that data would have inherited it perfectly.
Computational and statistical
Arising from non-representative samples and systematic measurement error. The CSBG allocation formula runs on estimated poverty counts believed to undercount rural populations, an effect COVID worsened. Agencies have described being funded on a headcount that does not reflect who is actually there — and that arithmetic then persists for years.
Human-cognitive
How people interpret and defer to system output. The one to watch here is automation bias: deferring to a recommendation because the system made it, even when you have full authority to overrule. It is the reason “a human reviews it” is not automatically a safeguard.

AI does not usually invent bias. It inherits it and applies it faster, more consistently, and at larger scale — which turns a tendency into a pattern.

Part 2

What a needs assessment cannot see

This is the most important idea in the module, and it limits what any bias-detection tool can do for you.

In one western North Carolina service area, copper landlines are being discontinued ahead of the broadband buildout that was supposed to replace them. For elderly residents whose medical alert devices and heart monitors run on those lines, in places with poor cell coverage, this is an acute safety crisis. It surfaced within the last couple of years.

It appeared nowhere in the prior Community Needs Assessment.

Not because the assessment was careless. Because residents were not asked, and would not have raised it unprompted. As one agency leader put it: if all you have ever had is cornbread, you don't know chocolate cake tastes good. People cannot report the absence of something they have never had.

Now consider what a bias-audit tool could actually do with that assessment. It could compare respondent demographics against census figures and flag an under-represented group. Useful. But it could not have found the landline crisis, because the gap was not in who answered — it was in what was asked. No amount of demographic weighting surfaces a question nobody posed.

What this means practically

A tool can tell you whose voices are thin in your sample. Only people — outreach workers, partners, residents in open conversation — can tell you what nobody thought to ask about. Treat an automated audit as one input, never as a clean bill of health.

Part 3

Allegheny County, in full

You met this case briefly in Module 1. It is worth the whole story, because every decision in it was defensible and the outcome was still bad. That is what makes it instructive rather than merely cautionary.

Allegheny County, Pennsylvania built a screening tool to help child-welfare staff assess maltreatment risk in incoming referrals. The goal was sound: overloaded screeners making high-stakes judgments under time pressure.

The problem is that child maltreatment is not directly measurable. There is no column in any database for it. So the designers used the closest available stand-ins — re-referral and foster-care placement. Reasonable proxies. Both correlate with the thing you care about.

What those proxies quietly encoded

  • Who gets reported in the first place. Re-referral measures contact with the reporting system, not harm. Families already under scrutiny generate more of it.
  • Who uses public services at all. The tool could only see data from residents who had touched public programs. Families wealthy enough to use private doctors, private therapists and private childcare were structurally close to invisible.
  • The result: a system that, in effect, conflated being poor with being a risk — not because anyone intended it, but because that is what the available data encoded.

Nobody in this story was careless or malicious. The failure happened at the moment a social problem was translated into something a computer could act on — and that translation is a policy decision, not a technical one.

Part 4

Is “a human reviews it” a real safeguard?

Honestly: sometimes. It depends entirely on how the tool is built and introduced, and the research cuts both ways.

When it works The reviewer has time, context the system lacks, an easy way to disagree, and no penalty for doing so. Overrides are recorded and looked at. Disagreement is treated as information about the tool, not as the reviewer being difficult.
When it doesn't The recommendation appears first and anchors the judgment. Overriding takes extra clicks and a written justification. Throughput is measured. The reviewer is new, or tired, or handling forty cases. A signature at the bottom of a screen is not oversight.

This is why the assistant / decider line from Module 1 matters so much. An assistive tool — one that helps a caseworker find the right rule in a policy manual — keeps the judgment with the person by design. That is the configuration with the strongest evidence behind it: a randomized trial found a 40 percent accuracy improvement, with the biggest gains on the hardest cases. The tool never decided anything.

Part 5

The one-page checklist

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is the reference standard — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. It was built over eighteen months with more than 240 organizations, and not one of them was a three-person Community Action Agency. So here it is compressed to what an agency your size can actually do. Five questions. If you cannot answer all five, you are not ready to adopt the tool.

Before adopting any AI tool

Run it against a real pitch

Here is a vendor email of the kind agencies are already receiving. Read it, then take the five questions one at a time and decide whether this pitch actually answers each one.

The pitch “CaseFlow AI plugs straight into your existing intake and uses machine learning to surface the clients most at risk of disengaging, so your caseworkers can prioritise outreach. Our engagement-risk score is trained on over 4 million service records from agencies nationwide. Everything is reviewed by your staff before action is taken — the human is always in the loop. Enterprise-grade security, SOC 2 compliant, hosted in the cloud. Most partners are live in under two weeks. Happy to set up a demo!”

Five for five

Nothing in that email is a lie, and the product may well work. But it answers none of the five questions, and it is built around exactly the use this course recommends against — a system that ranks people. The checklist did not need you to be technical. It needed you to notice what was missing.

Keep this

The five questions are in the Toolkit alongside the other tools, and the Library lists the governance guidance this project is writing for agencies and for the state.

Questions for any vendor

  • Where does our data go, and which country is it stored in?
  • Is anything we enter used to train your models? Can that be turned off in writing?
  • Can we have our data deleted on request, and how long does that take?
  • What happens to our data when the contract ends?
  • Who is liable if the tool produces an error that affects a client?
  • Has this been evaluated with organizations like ours, and can we see the results?

The procurement trap

When you buy a tool rather than build one, you can accidentally outsource a policy decision and never notice. What counts as a resolved case? What counts as a flagged risk? What is the threshold for “at risk of falling out of the program”? Those are your judgments, tied to your community and your mandate. A vendor who has already answered them inside a product has made them for you, and has made them the same way for every agency that buys it. Ask what definitions are baked in, and whether you can change them.

Part 6

Turning the frame on this course

Fair is fair. Here is where the advice in the preceding five modules is weakest.

  • No study examines AI adoption in the CSBG network specifically. Every piece of evidence cited here comes from an adjacent setting — Spanish community social services, US child welfare, SNAP and Medicaid navigation, nonprofits generally. The translation to Community Action is reasoned, not demonstrated.
  • We have not shown that time saved becomes client time. The whole case rests on hours freed from reporting going back into direct service. That substitution is assumed across this literature and has not been measured. It could just as easily absorb into other administrative work.
  • Early enthusiasm fades. In the strongest pilot on record, tool usage declined over time without deliberate organizational effort to sustain it. If your agency adopts something after this course, plan for the ninety-day mark, not the first week.
  • AI is not neutral for your clients. One agency director pointed out that AI and automation are already eliminating the entry-level manual jobs their participants depend on. A course telling agencies to adopt AI should say plainly that the same technology is reshaping the labour market those agencies exist to help people enter. Both things are true.
Part 7

Quick self-check

1. A bias-audit tool reports your CNA sample matches county demographics closely. What does that tell you?

The landline crisis was missed by a demographically fine assessment. Sample representativeness and question coverage are different problems, and only one of them is measurable this way.

2. What went wrong in the Allegheny County screening tool?

The code worked as designed. The failure was upstream, at the moment someone translated “maltreatment risk” into two measurable stand-ins — a policy choice that looked like a technical one.

3. Your vendor says a human always reviews the output, so bias isn't a concern. What's the follow-up question?

Review is only a safeguard when overriding is genuinely easy and disagreement is tracked as information about the tool. If overriding takes extra steps and a written justification, you have automation bias built into the workflow.

The five things that stick

  1. AI rarely invents bias. It inherits it and scales it. Systemic, statistical and human-cognitive — and the third one is about you, not the machine.
  2. A tool can show you who is missing from your data. It cannot show you what nobody asked about. That still takes people.
  3. The Allegheny failure was a policy decision disguised as a technical one. Watch the moment a human problem gets translated into a measurable proxy.
  4. Five questions before any tool: who owns it, what may it touch, how will we know it's wrong, what happens then, and can the user overrule it easily.
  5. Hold the honest version. The evidence here is borrowed from adjacent fields, the time-savings-to-client-hours link is unproven, and the same technology is reshaping the job market your clients are trying to enter.
